**Most silicone is never recycled.** The industry group Silicones Europe says most silicone waste "ends up in landfills or is used for energy recovery". Silicone lasts for decades, which is great in use but a problem as waste.

## Where does silicone waste go today?

- **Landfill.** Silicone does not break down, so it stays there.
- **Burning to make energy.** This turns silicone into carbon dioxide, water and silica (a fine white powder). Researchers note this is often preferred to landfill.
- **Recycling.** A small share: an estimated 35,000 to 45,000 tonnes in 2024, out of about 3 million tonnes made.

A 2026 environmental report on silicone strips shows the usual picture. It assumes **73% is burned and 27% is landfilled**, and says the product cannot be ground up and reused.

## Why is so little silicone recycled?

**1. It is hard to collect.** Silicone is spread across millions of small products, from cables and seals to kitchenware. It is often stuck to other materials, so clean waste is rare.

**2. It cannot be melted.** Grinding lowers quality. Chemical recycling restores it but needs specialist plants. (See [silicone recycling explained](/insights/silicone-recycling-explained).)

**3. The industry is small.** A 2024 research review counts only one to two dozen small and medium-sized recyclers worldwide.

**4. Rules may tighten.** Chemical recycling produces small ring-shaped molecules called D4, D5 and D6. In 2018 the EU listed them as "substances of very high concern". Silicones Europe and researchers warn that stricter rules worldwide could make recycling harder.

## What is changing?

Big silicone makers are investing. Elkem has tested chemical recycling in France and in 2025 launched 100% recycled silicone for labels. In 2023, Dow announced a recycling plant in Kentucky with Circusil.

Brands also want recycled content they can **prove**. Schemes such as GRS and ISCC PLUS check the paper trail from waste to product (see [GRS vs ISCC PLUS vs SCS](/insights/grs-vs-iscc-plus-vs-scs)).
