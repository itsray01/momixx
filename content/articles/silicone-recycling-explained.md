**Yes, silicone can be recycled, but not by melting it down.** Cured silicone rubber is a *thermoset*: once its chains are cross-linked, heating does not turn it back into a liquid. That rules out the melt-and-remould route used for most plastics. Instead there are two other routes, **mechanical** and **chemical** recycling, and they produce very different results.

## Why can't silicone be recycled like plastic?

Most everyday plastics, such as PET bottles, are **thermoplastics**. They soften when heated and can be re-shaped. Silicone rubber is cured into a permanent three-dimensional network. That network is what makes silicone so heat-resistant and durable, and it is also why ordinary recycling lines cannot process it.

## What is mechanical recycling?

Mechanical recycling **shreds and grinds** silicone waste, sometimes at cryogenic temperatures, into a fine powder. The powder is then used as a **filler**, mixed into new silicone or other materials.

It is simple and uses relatively little energy. But a 2024 review in *Polymers* found that adding ground silicone powder to new high-temperature-vulcanising silicone cut its tensile and tear strength by about **20%**. Quality cannot be kept up over two or more loops, so the authors call it **downcycling**. Industry is working on improving it: Elkem reported a proof of concept in 2025 that re-incorporated more than 50% of mechanically recycled HCR.

## What is chemical recycling?

Chemical recycling, or **depolymerisation**, breaks silicone's long chains back into small molecules. Heat and catalysts (commonly strong acids or bases, at temperatures from room temperature up to around 180 °C) shift silicone's natural ring–chain equilibrium. The chains unzip into **cyclic siloxanes**, often called DMC, which are continuously removed and collected.

Those cyclic siloxanes can be **purified and polymerised again** into new silicone. Because the material is rebuilt from its molecular building blocks, the researchers describe the output as **"virgin-grade"**, and the loop as closed. Silicones Europe says the industry's goal is to recycle waste back into the cyclic monomers D4, D5 and D6.

## Mechanical vs chemical recycling at a glance

| Aspect | Mechanical | Chemical (depolymerisation) |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | Grind into powder | Break chains into cyclic siloxanes |
| Output | Filler powder | Purified monomers → new silicone |
| Quality | Lower strength; "downcycling" | Comparable to virgin material |
| Repeatable? | Limited | Yes, in principle |
| Complexity | Low | Higher: needs chemistry, purification and controls |

## How does Momixx recycle silicone?

Momixx uses the **chemical route**, in five steps:

1. **Collect scrap:** factory offcuts (post-industrial) and used products (post-consumer).
2. **Break it down:** depolymerisation splits the silicone back into its molecular building blocks.
3. **Purify:** the recovered cyclic siloxanes (DMC) are cleaned.
4. **Make silicone oil:** purified DMC is rebuilt into silicone oil, the same base used for virgin silicone.
5. **New silicone:** we compound the oil into new, high-performance silicone, traceable batch by batch.

## How big is silicone recycling today?

It is still small. The same review estimates that around **35,000 to 45,000 tonnes** of silicone were chemically recycled in 2024, out of **2.9 to 3.0 million tonnes** produced. (The paper gives slightly different figures in different sections; this is its widest range.) The sector is made up of one to two dozen small and medium-sized companies, and the authors call it "still in its infancy".
