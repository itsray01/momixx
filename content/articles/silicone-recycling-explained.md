**Yes, silicone can be recycled, but not by melting it down.** Once silicone rubber has set, heat will not turn it back into a liquid. There are two other ways, **mechanical** and **chemical**, with very different results.

## Why can't silicone be recycled like plastic?

Most plastics, such as drinks bottles, soften when heated and can be reshaped. When silicone sets, its molecules lock into a permanent mesh. That mesh makes it tough and heat-resistant, but normal recycling plants cannot handle it.

## What is mechanical recycling?

Mechanical recycling **grinds** silicone waste into a powder, which is mixed into new materials as a **filler**. It is simple and uses little energy, but a 2024 research review found it made new silicone about **20% weaker**. Quality drops with each round, so the authors call it **downcycling**. In 2025, Elkem reported an early trial that reused more than 50% ground-up rubber in new material.

## What is chemical recycling?

Chemical recycling, or **depolymerisation**, uses heat and chemicals to "unzip" silicone's long molecules into **DMC** (a liquid building block of silicone). The DMC is **cleaned and rebuilt** into new silicone. Because it starts again from the same building blocks, researchers describe the result as being as good as new. The industry group Silicones Europe says this is where the industry wants to go.

## Mechanical vs chemical recycling at a glance

| | Mechanical (grinding) | Chemical (breaking down) |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | A filler powder | Building blocks for new silicone |
| Quality | Weaker ("downcycling") | Comparable to new |
| Can it be repeated? | Not well; quality drops | Yes, in principle |
| How hard is it? | Simple | Harder: needs chemistry, cleaning and careful control |

## How much silicone is recycled today?

Not much yet. The same review estimates **35,000 to 45,000 tonnes** were chemically recycled in 2024, out of about **3 million tonnes** made. (This is the widest of the ranges the paper gives.) Only one to two dozen small and medium-sized firms do this work, and the authors say the industry is "still in its infancy".
